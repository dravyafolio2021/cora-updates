# The Agency Client Onboarding Playbook

**A practical system to turn signed proposals into organized, confident client delivery.**

For agency founders, account managers, project leads, and operations teams.

---

## Introduction - The sale is not the handoff

A client can sign the proposal, pay the invoice, and still arrive inside delivery as a stranger.

Sales remembers the nuance. The founder remembers the promises made on the final call. The client assumes the delivery team already knows everything. Then the project starts and the team asks the client to repeat what they already explained.

That is where weak onboarding begins.

The purpose of onboarding is not to send more forms. It is to transfer context, confirm the operating rules, collect what genuinely blocks the work, and create enough clarity for the team to deliver without repeatedly going back to the client.

A strong onboarding system should answer five questions early:

1. Why did this client hire us?
2. What exactly did we agree to deliver?
3. What do we need from the client to begin?
4. Who can make decisions and approve work?
5. What happens next, and who owns it?

This playbook builds that system step by step.

# Chapter 1 - Onboarding Is a Handoff Problem Before It Is a Form Problem

Most agencies try to fix onboarding by improving the client form. That helps, but it rarely fixes the root problem.

The first failure usually happens before the client sees the form: **sales context does not survive the handoff into delivery.**

A proposal may show deliverables, price, and timeline. It usually does not capture the full story behind the sale: the client's internal pressure, what they already tried, what worried them during negotiation, which result matters most, or what promises changed the buyer's confidence.

If the delivery team receives only the proposal, they inherit the contract but not the context.

## What must survive the sales-to-delivery handoff

### 1. Why the client bought

Write the business problem in one or two sentences.

Bad:

> Social media management retainer.

Better:

> The brand has consistent organic content but weak paid-social creative volume. They hired us to increase the pace and quality of campaign-ready creatives before the festive sales period.

The second version tells the delivery team what the work is actually trying to solve.

### 2. What was actually promised

Document meaningful commitments made outside the proposal.

Examples:
- "We will review the first batch together before the normal approval process begins."
- "The founder wants one weekly summary, not daily updates."
- "We agreed to prioritize the new product line during the first month."

A promise does not stop being real because it happened on a call.

### 3. What success means to the client

Do not assume the stated deliverable is the success metric.

A client buying a website may care most about launch speed. Another may care about conversion clarity. Another may care about giving the sales team a credible page they can share in enterprise outreach.

Write down the buyer's actual definition of a good outcome.

### 4. What can block delivery

List known dependencies before the project begins:
- access;
- approvals;
- legal review;
- missing brand assets;
- a product launch date;
- an external developer;
- a founder who gives final approval;
- an ad account currently under review.

Make blockers visible while there is still time to plan around them.

### 5. Who can decide

Every engagement needs one clearly identified decision owner.

That does not mean only one person can give feedback. It means one person has the authority to resolve conflicting feedback, approve final work, and authorize material changes.

## The one-page engagement brief

Before sending the client another questionnaire, create an internal brief that answers:

- **Problem:** What business problem created this engagement?
- **Outcome:** What should improve if the work succeeds?
- **Scope:** What are we responsible for?
- **Owners:** Who owns delivery, approvals, and key client inputs?
- **Next milestone:** What is the first meaningful result the client should see?

Add links to the proposal, call notes, signed agreement, and known dependencies underneath.

### Internal handoff test

Ask one question:

> If the salesperson disappeared for two weeks, would the delivery team still understand what was sold, why it matters, and what should happen next?

If the answer is no, the handoff is incomplete.

# Chapter 2 - Make Scope, Responsibilities, and Change Rules Visible

Scope creep rarely starts with one unreasonable request. It starts with a sequence of reasonable requests that nobody classifies.

"Can you add one more variation?"

"Can the team join this call?"

"Can we also change the landing page?"

Individually, each request can feel too small to discuss commercially. Collectively, they can change the project.

The solution is not to become rigid. The solution is to make the original baseline visible enough that everyone can see when the work changes.

PMI scope-management guidance treats the approved baseline as the reference point used to assess later changes. That principle translates cleanly to agency work: define the baseline before execution, then make changes explicit instead of letting them enter delivery invisibly.

## Use an operating scope, not only a deliverables list

A deliverables list says what you will make.

An operating scope explains how the engagement will work.

Include these six elements:

### Outcome

What business result is the work intended to support?

### Included work

List the actual outputs and recurring responsibilities.

### Exclusions

Name predictable requests that are not included.

Do not try to list every imaginable exclusion. Focus on the areas clients commonly misunderstand.

### Client responsibilities

Examples:
- provide access by an agreed date;
- nominate the final approval owner;
- provide product/legal information;
- return feedback within the agreed review window.

### Agency responsibilities

Examples:
- deliver the agreed output;
- surface blockers early;
- maintain the agreed reporting rhythm;
- document material scope changes before execution.

### Change rule

Define how the team handles requests that alter scope, timeline, capacity, or responsibility.

## Classify changes before committing

Use four buckets:

### Clarification

The client needs help understanding work already included.

Action: handle inside scope.

### Revision

The client wants an agreed deliverable adjusted within the revision policy.

Action: handle according to the agreed revision rules.

### Substitution

The client wants to replace one planned item with another.

Action: confirm the trade-off and update the plan.

### Additional scope

The client wants more work without removing anything from the current plan.

Action: assess the impact before the team commits.

## Use three options instead of a hard no

When a request changes the plan, give the client choices.

### Replace

> We can add this and remove X from the current plan.

### Extend

> We can include this, but it will move the delivery date to X.

### Add

> We can keep the original plan and add this as additional work for X.

This keeps the agency helpful without making flexibility invisible.

## Better scope language

Instead of:

> That's out of scope.

Use:

> Yes, we can help with that. It changes the current plan, so let me show you the cleanest options before the team starts working on it.

Same boundary. Better client experience.

# Chapter 3 - Collect Information Without Building a Form Maze

A long intake form can feel organized while still producing poor context.

The better question is not:

> What can we ask the client?

It is:

> What will the team do differently because we know this?

Every question should have a job.

Keep a question when its answer:
- changes the strategy;
- unlocks a dependency;
- assigns ownership;
- prevents a predictable misunderstanding;
- helps the team make a decision.

Delete or postpone the rest.

## Split client information into four groups

### 1. Business context

Ask:
- What does the company sell?
- Who is the primary customer?
- What changed recently?
- Why does this project matter now?
- What would make the engagement feel successful?

### 2. Commercial context

Ask only what matters to the work:
- primary offer;
- pricing or average order/project value where relevant;
- acquisition channels;
- sales process;
- important campaign dates;
- relevant seasonality.

### 3. Brand / product context

Collect:
- brand guidelines;
- product/service information;
- existing research;
- prior creative or campaign examples;
- approved claims;
- asset library;
- customer insight already available.

### 4. Decision context

Identify:
- day-to-day client contact;
- final approval owner;
- finance contact if needed;
- technical contact if needed;
- people who should be consulted but do not approve.

## Do not make the client repeat the sales call

Carry known information forward.

If sales already captured the goals, target audience, existing stack, or internal constraints, prefill those fields and ask the client to confirm rather than asking them to start again.

That single change makes onboarding feel dramatically more intentional.

## Use progressive intake

You do not need every piece of information on day one.

Split the requests into:

### Required before kickoff

Information needed to have a useful kickoff conversation.

### Required before execution

Access or assets that block the first deliverable.

### Can arrive later

Items that matter later in the engagement but do not block the first milestone.

This keeps onboarding lighter without sacrificing rigor.

# Chapter 4 - Collect Access Without Creating a Security Mess

Access collection creates friction because agencies often treat every account as a password problem.

It is usually an authorization problem instead.

NIST defines least privilege as giving users only the access needed to complete assigned tasks. That principle is useful for agency onboarding too: request the minimum access required, prefer delegated or role-based permissions, and remove access when the engagement ends.

## Use this access hierarchy

### 1. Delegated / collaborator access

Prefer this whenever the platform supports it.

Examples:
- Shopify collaborator access;
- Meta Business roles;
- Google Ads manager access;
- Google Analytics/Search Console user permissions;
- CMS user roles.

### 2. Role-based team access

If several agency team members need access, assign the appropriate role rather than sharing one broad credential.

### 3. Shared credentials only when unavoidable

If a system genuinely does not support delegated access, use a secure password manager or another controlled method.

Do not collect passwords in ordinary chat threads or spreadsheets when safer options exist.

## Separate access into three states

### Needed now

Blocks kickoff or the first milestone.

### Needed before execution

Does not block kickoff but blocks a later task.

### Optional / later

Useful for future optimization but not required for current work.

This prevents the client from receiving a 20-item access list when only four items actually matter today.

## Build an access tracker

For each required system track:

| Platform | Access needed | Owner | Status | Blocks | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shopify | Collaborator | Client ecommerce lead | Pending | Product page work | Request sent |
| Meta | Partner access | Client marketing lead | Complete | Paid social | - |
| GA4 | Viewer/analyst | Client marketing lead | Complete | Reporting | - |

The point is not the spreadsheet. The point is making dependencies visible.

## Never say only "waiting on client"

Write the actual blocker.

Bad:

> Waiting on client.

Better:

> Waiting on Shopify collaborator access from Priya. Product page implementation cannot start until access is granted.

Specific blockers create action. Generic blockers create frustration.

# Chapter 5 - Run a Kickoff Meeting That Produces Decisions

A kickoff is not a presentation about the agency.

It is a working session that removes ambiguity before execution begins.

Atlassian's project kickoff guidance emphasizes aligning purpose, roles, responsibilities, success markers, and next steps. That is exactly what an agency kickoff should do.

## Prepare before the call

Send a short pre-read containing:
- engagement objective;
- agreed scope;
- attendees and roles;
- current missing information/access;
- proposed first milestone;
- agenda.

Do not spend the first 20 minutes reading information everyone could have reviewed beforehand.

## A practical 35-minute kickoff agenda

### 0-5 min - Why this engagement exists

Ask:
- Why now?
- What changed before the client hired us?
- Which outcome matters most?

### 5-12 min - Confirm scope and responsibilities

Review:
- included work;
- exclusions;
- client responsibilities;
- agency responsibilities.

Resolve ambiguity immediately.

### 12-20 min - Confirm information and access

Review only unresolved prerequisites.

Do not turn the meeting into a password collection session.

### 20-27 min - Agree how the relationship will operate

Confirm:
- primary communication channel;
- update rhythm;
- final approval owner;
- expected feedback window;
- escalation path.

### 27-33 min - First delivery milestone

Show:
- what the client will see first;
- who owns it;
- due date;
- dependencies;
- what happens immediately afterward.

### 33-35 min - Repeat the next actions

End with:
- action;
- owner;
- date.

A kickoff is successful when everyone leaves knowing what happens next.

## Send the recap quickly

The recap should include:
- decisions made;
- actions and owners;
- due dates;
- unresolved blockers;
- first milestone.

Do not send a transcript. Send a decision record.

# Chapter 6 - Set Communication and Approval Rules Before You Need Them

Most client communication problems are not caused by the wrong communication tool.

They happen because nobody agreed how decisions would be made.

## Define four operating rules

### 1. Primary communication lane

Choose the channel for normal project communication.

The team can still use calls or email when appropriate, but important decisions should return to the agreed source of truth.

### 2. Update cadence

Do not make the client ask:

> Any update?

Choose a predictable rhythm.

Examples:
- weekly Friday status note;
- twice-weekly update during a launch sprint;
- milestone-based updates for project work.

### 3. Approval owner

Name the final decision maker.

If five people comment and nobody owns the decision, the agency receives feedback but not direction.

### 4. Feedback rule

Ask for consolidated feedback where practical.

If three client stakeholders send three different messages, the agency should not decide which stakeholder outranks the others.

## Use a simple approval record

For meaningful decisions record:
- deliverable;
- version;
- decision;
- approval owner;
- date;
- important notes.

This does not need to become bureaucracy. It needs to prevent the sentence:

> I thought we approved the other version.

## Escalate decisions, not emotion

When an issue gets stuck, frame it around the decision needed.

Instead of:

> We have been waiting for feedback for four days.

Use:

> To keep Thursday's launch date, we need final approval on Option B by 2 PM today. If approval arrives later, we will move launch to Monday.

That turns frustration into a business choice.

# Chapter 7 - Design the First 30 Days to Build Confidence

The client should not feel the project disappear after kickoff.

The first month should make three things visible:

1. the team understood the problem;
2. the work is moving;
3. the client knows what happens next.

## Day 0 - Agreement and welcome

- confirm signed scope;
- confirm commercial start condition;
- send welcome note;
- create internal handoff brief;
- identify owners.

## Days 1-3 - Intake and access

- collect only the information needed now;
- request required access;
- resolve blockers;
- schedule kickoff;
- prepare first milestone.

## Days 2-5 - Kickoff and project setup

- run kickoff;
- record decisions;
- confirm communication rules;
- assign first milestone;
- update project workspace.

## Week 1 - Show visible progress

The first milestone should prove that the engagement has moved from administration into delivery.

Examples:
- first strategy map;
- first creative direction;
- analytics baseline;
- initial CRO audit;
- first campaign build;
- first wireframe;
- first reporting framework.

Do not optimize for spectacle. Optimize for clarity and momentum.

## Week 2 - Run the first structured review

Review:
- what has been completed;
- what the team learned;
- decisions needed;
- scope changes;
- blockers;
- next milestone.

## Week 3 - Stabilize the rhythm

Look for recurring friction:
- approvals always late;
- unclear responsibility;
- access still missing;
- too many communication channels;
- client asks for updates before scheduled reporting.

Fix the operating problem rather than accepting it as normal.

## Week 4 - Run an onboarding retrospective

Ask internally:
- What did the client ask twice?
- Which access should we have requested earlier?
- Which question added no value?
- Which responsibility was unclear?
- Where did sales context get lost?
- Which step should become a reusable template or automation?

Then update the onboarding system before the next client arrives.

# Chapter 8 - Build an Internal Handoff That Does Not Depend on Memory

Good client onboarding can still fail if the internal team receives work in fragments.

The handoff should create one connected operating picture.

## Internal readiness checklist

Before delivery begins, confirm:

- [ ] Approved proposal / agreement is accessible
- [ ] Outcome is written in plain language
- [ ] Included scope is clear
- [ ] Exclusions are clear
- [ ] Commercial terms are understood
- [ ] Client responsibilities are visible
- [ ] Agency responsibilities are assigned
- [ ] Approval owner is identified
- [ ] Important sales promises are documented
- [ ] Access requirements have owners and statuses
- [ ] First milestone has an owner and date
- [ ] Communication cadence is scheduled
- [ ] Known risks are visible

## Keep the engagement context connected

Agencies create avoidable work when:
- the proposal lives in one tool;
- client information lives in a form;
- access requests live in chat;
- approvals live in email;
- tasks live in a project board;
- invoices live somewhere else.

The team spends time reconstructing the relationship instead of running it.

The operating goal is simple:

> Keep the agreement, client context, responsibilities, decisions, delivery, and commercial history close enough that the team can understand the engagement without searching six systems.

That is also where Cora fits naturally: not as another isolated tool, but as a connected operating layer for the work between winning the client and delivering the result.

## The rule to remember

**The client should never need to manage your agency for you.**

A strong onboarding system removes uncertainty before it becomes follow-up, rework, scope conflict, or client anxiety.

Start with the handoff. Make the agreement visible. Ask only what matters. Request access responsibly. Use kickoff to make decisions. Define communication before it becomes noisy. Show progress early. Improve the system after every client.

That is onboarding.
